Can you learn a language by reading books?

Reading helps, but it is one part of learning a language and not the whole of it. It does not replace speaking practice or a course. This guide describes a routine that makes the reading part count, using Bilingua Reader as the example.

What reading gives you, and what it does not

When you read a real book you meet many words in context, you see the same grammar patterns again and again, and you set your own pace. That is useful for vocabulary and for understanding. What reading does not give you is speaking practice, someone to correct your own sentences, or a plan that covers everything. Treat it as one routine among others, and add conversation or a course for the rest.

A routine in five steps

  1. Pick a book you can manage. Choose something short, or a story you already know in your own language, so that the plot helps you guess. Avoid starting with a hard classic. Bilingua Reader comes with two short starter books if you want to try the method first.
  2. Read with the translation under each sentence. Read the original sentence first and look at the translation only when you need it. The hide option blurs one side until you tap, which turns each sentence into a small recall exercise.
  3. Save the words that matter. Double-tap a word to save it, or triple-tap a sentence to save the sentence and its translation. Saved words are highlighted wherever they appear, and turn green once you know them.
  4. Review them. The built-in flashcards use spaced repetition with four answers (Again, Hard, Good, Easy), a daily target of new cards and an optional reminder. If you prefer Anki, words and sentences can go to AnkiDroid with spoken clips, and the sync runs both ways.
  5. Listen as well as read. The app can read the book aloud sentence by sentence in the language you are learning, even with the screen off. It uses the voices installed on your phone, so the quality depends on them.

A sample week

This is only a suggestion, not a promise of results. Read for fifteen minutes on three or four days, saving a handful of words each time, and do one short flashcard session on the days in between. At the end of the week, reread a page you found hard at the start and see how much of it you now follow without the translation. Adjust the amounts to your own time.

Keep sessions short

Ten to twenty minutes with a book you enjoy is easier to repeat than one long session. If you lose the thread easily, the optional focus mode dims everything except the paragraph you are on and reminds you to take a break after a time you choose.

What to add

  • Speaking with other people, in a class, a language exchange or a tutor session, because reading will not train you to speak.
  • A course or textbook if you want grammar explained in order. The app can show grammar notes for a word, but it is not a course.
  • Writing, with someone to give feedback, if you need to produce the language.

Questions and answers

Can reading alone make me fluent?

No. Reading helps with vocabulary and understanding, but fluency also needs speaking and listening practice. Use reading as one part of a routine that includes conversation or a course.

Should I keep the translation visible all the time?

It is a matter of taste and level. A good habit is to read the original sentence first and check the translation only when you need it. The hide option blurs one side until you tap, so you can test yourself.

Which books can I use?

EPUB and PDF files without DRM, from your phone or from OPDS catalogues such as Project Gutenberg. Two short starter books come with the app. DRM-protected books cannot be opened.
