An EPUB reader with sentence-by-sentence translation

An EPUB reader that translates can mean three quite different things. Which one you want depends on whether you are reading to understand a text or to learn the language it is written in. Here is how the three approaches differ and where each one fits.

1. Tap a word to translate it

You read the original and tap the word or sentence you do not know. A small pop-up shows the translation and you carry on. This works well when you already read the language and only meet an unknown word now and then. The cost is that the translation is only there for what you tap, so a text with many unknown words means many interruptions.

2. Translate the whole book

The reader produces a version of the book in your own language and you read that. This is the quickest way to find out what a book says. It is a poor fit for learning, because you are no longer reading the language you want to learn, and the quality of a machine translation of a whole book is only as good as the engine behind it.

3. Show every sentence with its translation

The original stays on the page, and the translation sits under each sentence. You can read the original first and look down when you need to, so the foreign text is always in front of you and the help is one glance away. The trade-off is more text on the screen, and the translation quality is that of the engine you choose.

How Bilingua Reader does it

Bilingua Reader is built around the third approach and keeps the first one as an extra. A chapter is split into sentences, and sentences are translated as they come close to the screen, so you can start reading at once. The translation can sit on its own line, on the same line, or be switched off, and a hide option blurs one side until you tap it. Long-press any word to see its meaning, the sentence it came from and that sentence translated, and save it for flashcards or Anki.

Translation runs on your phone by default, with offline language packs for 59 languages. Online providers such as Google Translate, DeepL, MyMemory, Azure AI Foundry, OpenRouter or your own LibreTranslate server are optional and can be set for all books or pinned to one book. Each translation is stored per book, so a book is only translated once.

A short test before you decide

Open the same chapter with the translation on its own line, on the same line and hidden, and read a page in each. Most people know within a few minutes which layout lets them read the original without being pulled into the translation. Try an offline pack and an online provider on the same page too, and compare how natural the sentences sound to you.

Which approach should you pick?

  • If you want to understand a text and not learn its language, translating the whole book is the fastest route.
  • If you are comfortable in the language and only need the odd word, tapping is enough.
  • If you want to learn from real books and keep the original in front of you, show every sentence with its translation.

Questions and answers

Does the original text stay visible?

Yes. The translation is added under each sentence, and the original text stays where it is. You can also switch the translation off or hide it until you tap.

Which translation engine does it use?

By default the on-device models, which work offline. You can switch to Google Translate, DeepL, MyMemory, Azure AI Foundry, OpenRouter or your own LibreTranslate server in Settings. An online provider receives the sentence being translated.

Can it open a book with DRM?

No. DRM-protected books are encrypted and cannot be opened. The app reads EPUB and PDF files without DRM, from your phone or from OPDS catalogues such as Project Gutenberg.
